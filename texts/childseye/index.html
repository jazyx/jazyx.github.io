Childseye

by James Newton

BBC Wildlife Magazine Award for Nature Writing — Best Amateur 1994

"What is it going to become?" Matthew is three years old. The world fascinates him. A non-stop question-mark, three feet tall — that's what he is. He's recently traded in the all-encompassing question "Why?" for this new, and more enigmatic one.

"What is it going to become?"

It must have started when I tried to explain an acorn to him. The forest near our house is full of oak and sweet chestnut. Last autumn I took him with me to collect a basketful of sweet chestnuts to roast. I showed him what we were looking for: the green hedgehog balls and the shiny brown chestnuts nestling inside. He didn't much appreciate the prickles, but enjoyed toddling off to find loose chestnuts. Soon the basket was full. Matthew's contribution was various, to say the least. It was too much to ask him to choose only those of a larger size. From then on, whenever we went into the forest, he would delight in picking up and pointing out chestnuts.

And then the thought struck me. Despite the large number of acorns lying around, he never brought me one. It was as if they were invisible to him. I had not spoken about them, had not given him any to touch. I tried an experiment. I showed him flints, and how easy it was to break chips off them. I showed him dead leaves, their different shapes, and how they crumble into dust when they're dry. I showed him twigs and fallen branches. I showed him how the ground is littered with small discoveries. His explorations were punctuated with little chirrups of success. But I didn't show him any acorns. And he continued not to see any.

And suddenly I felt the responsibility of parenthood like an immense weight. What I do not show my son, he finds hard to see. What if I left him to make all his discoveries for himself? How much less would he learn? How much longer would it take? How much simpler would his understanding of the world be?

Not only did I show him an acorn, I filled a spaghetti jar with drainage stones and earth, and planted the acorn. I put it on his bedroom windowsill, and let him help me water it. I bought a book with pictures to answer "What's it going to become?" And now, when visitors come, Matthew will proudly, and with infinite precaution, carry his spaghetti jar and baby oak from visitor to visitor, to have it admired.

I bought a book. I bought book after book. I'm still buying books. Trees, flowers, insects, birds and mammals. Matthew wants to know the name of everything. But footprints is the book he loves the best. More than likely, this has something to do with the squdgy quality of mud.

"It's too muddy to play there, Matthew, you'll get all dirty."

"No, but it's to look for footprints." He crouches down in his 'nature study' position, waving a finger at the height of his nose, vaguely aimed at what he has seen. "You can show me in the book?"

"Well, if it's to look for footprints, I suppose..." (laundry again tonight).

The most common animal prints are dog, but the forest is a favourite place for horse-riders, so we find plenty of hoof prints, too. On the side of the lake, we find duck and moorhen, and, once, a roe deer came to drink. When it snows, the ground becomes a scribble of small birds and mammals. Like a prehistoric hunter, Matthew is learning to read, without the help of an ABC.

"What made this mark, Matthew?" (In the corner of a field trampled to mud around a chipped enamel bath.)

"Ah... I don't know."

"Was it a big animal or a small animal?"

"Big animal."

"As big as a dog? As big as a horse?"

"Big as a horse."

"Can you see any animals as big as a horse?"

"A cow! It's a cow! Look! Lots and lots of cow prints! Look!"

"And what were the cows doing here? Why is it all muddy here?"

"Ah... I don't know. To have a bath?"

"Let's wait and watch."

Learning patience. Normally Matthew is a bundle of energy, but when we're 'watching', he stays still as still, only turning his face to mine from time to time, to be sure that I'm being still too. Gradually the cows drift across the field towards the bath. Snuffling and jostling around the bath.

"What are they doing, Matthew?"

"Drinking," he laughs. "They're drinking from the bath!"

Later, he complains, "I can't find cow footprints in my book." There are lots of prints he can't find in his book — all the common ones. Dogs, horses, logging tractors, motorcycles using the forests as a playground. All the signs of human labour and leisure. The tracks in his book are often very hard to see.

We found a newt on a rainy day, dragging its tail across the sodden path. The trail was clear at first: four toes at the front, five at the back, and a shallow rut for the tail. The wind was tossing great dollops of rain-water from the trees, bombarding the trail, effacing it.

"Look!" says Matthew, indignant. "What's it going to become?"

A memory wiped from the mud. The motorcycle tracks close by were deep enough to resist.

Matthew's latest discovery is death. A russet-coloured branch on a green tree. A sparrow squashed on the road, feathers fluttered by the breeze. An ant that stops moving when he investigates too closely.

I don't yet know how to answer him when he looks from the ant to me, and then to the wealth of nature all around him and asks, "What's it going to become?"

I only know that I have a weighty responsibility to show him the infinity of creation, so that, later, he will be able to understand even those things that I find hard to see.
