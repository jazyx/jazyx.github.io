The Raccoon

It was a moment of recklessness that changed his life.

With his two friends, he was crossing the highway, the day everyone else was going on holiday. A highways where the cars were moving at 80 kmh, with both lanes full in both directions. The three of them said "Dare you!" and they ran across. He wanted to show he was the boss, so he ran more slowly. And stopped to faze the driver of the car that was bearing straight down on him.

He expected the car to slow down. That's what had happened all the other times. Frantic horn, frantic brakes, the driver's fist raised in an empty menace, and the whole line of cars behind which had to slow down. And then, that's it. On the road, the traffic would continue, and he would be on the far side, proud of his little victory over his fear.

"See the guy lose it, eh? Got a car, thinks he can drive."

And his friends would say: "Tonio, you're the boss, man. You ain't afraid of nothing."

But this time, no, no brakes. A foot on the accelerator pedal. And he was the one who lost it. He just had time to see the driver's face light up in a smile. Tonio scuttled to the side of the road, ashen. Interior conflict: the adrenaline like knives in his veins, and the obligation to make his friends believe that he was calm. He was just about to insult the guy, this asshole driver, to call him an assassin, when he heard a screech of brakes behind him.

He turned round. The car was there, at an angle on the grass verge, black skid marks on the road and a deep trench gouged out of the grass by the tyres.

"You hit the wrong pedal, pillock," Tonio said to himself. "So bloody frightened, you hit the wrong pedal." And he felt his courage flow back into him. He gave a chuckle, as a sign to his friends.

But the car door opened, and the man who got out was still smiling. He leaned his back against the car and lit a cigarette. Taking his time. And only afterwards did he bother to raise his head and notice Tonio, who was staring at him.

"You like taking risks," he said. "That's good. Come here."

His voice carried over the noise of the traffic. And yet he wasn't speaking loudly. A voice that has been developed, shaped, a voice that is used to making itself heard.

"Pillock," Tonio throws at him, without moving, like a challenge. He is aware of his two friends moving close behind him. Deadeye and the Handyman.

"Too bad," the man said. "I must have overestimated you. I thought you had the makings of a leader. I see that your friends, there, they're not afraid of me."

Tonio felt obliged to move forward.

"What you want, eh? You looking for something?"

"Perhaps. But on second thoughts, no. I was going to make you a proposition, but you just haven't got it." He turned to re-open the car door.

Tonio felt disoriented. He was no longer in control of the situation. He was going to lose face. Whatever he did, he must not turn to face the others. One step, painfully, forward. And another. His hand now on the car door, to prevent it from opening further. So close to this stranger that he could feel the heat of his body.

"Who do you think you are, to talk to me like that?" A sharp sign to his mates, without looking at them, to make them come close, to create a threat. To show them who was the boss.

"Folks call me the Raccoon," the man said. "You know why?"

"Don't give a shit," said Tonio, almost in the man's face.

"No," said the Raccoon. "I didn't think so. Are you interested in making a pile of money?"

The last sentence, he almost whispered it. A little quieter and Tonio would not have heard it. A secret, handcrafted for him alone. A secret, which separated him from his friends. He took a step back, to move outside the intimacy, to join ranks with his friends. Defeated.

"How much?"

"That depends entirely on you. On the risks that you are willing to take."

The Raccoon let time go by. The torrent of cars on the highway orchestrated a loud silence. Tonio refused to be the first to speak. Swallowed. Waited for the Game Over sign.

"Here," said the Raccoon. "Call me."

He wrote a phone number on his cigarette packet, and beneath it, the word "Backgammon".

"Can you read?"

"You crazy, man?"

"Well, what's written there?"

"Your phone number. Sheesh!"

"Read it."

Tonio read it.

"And that word, there. Do you know what it means?"

"Backgammon. So what?"

"So you'll call me when you know what it means."

The Raccoon put the cigarette packet back in his pocket. Tonio was not expecting that. He almost held out his hand to take it. He had to stop himself from blinking. To hold in the number that was escaping him already. To hide the shame that he had been played with.

He got back into the car. Tonio realized, as if in a dream, that there was a woman inside. And him, he was outside, empty-handed. He watched for a moment as the car drove off, manoeuvering on the grass verge. Flashing an authoritarian indicator light. Tonio at least had the satisfaction of seeing the Raccoon obliged to wait for a gap in the traffic.

He turned towards his friends.

"You see that?" And he saw in their eyes, that yes, they had seen.

"That's backgammon?"

Tonio feels that he is being mocked. An old board game made of worn cardboard, twisted at one corner. On one side, printed in dull colours, a draughts board. On the other, a backgammon board: 12 long thin triangles, alternatively red and white pointing like arrows at 12 others. The same sad colours. A box of pieces for playing draughts and 4 dice.

"Man, this is cheap. This is what he tried to get me all wound up about, the bleeder."

He turns the board over, draughts board, backgammon board, unable to believe his senses.

"So how do you play it?"

It's old Mary-Lynn who found it for him, among the things that her missing husband left behind. Missing, not missed.

"You know, Toni, me, I never played. It's with the dice, like, like a horse race. You make 'em run, the pieces, like little horses."

"A kids' game. He rolled me over with a bleeding kid's game. Shit, is he ever going to pay for that!"

Beside himself, but with all his violence within. The Raccoon's phone number flashing in his brain like a faulty neon sign.

In the evening, in the pub, surrounded by his mates, he took one too many drinks before taking out his cell phone. On the side of the highway, he had believed in the Raccoon. The ease with which this stranger had thrown him off balance had disturbed him. He had let himself believe in this vague promise of "a pile of money". He had let the idea take root in his mind. But here, warm, surrounded by all these familiar faces, with the shabby board game printed in faded colours as evidence, it was the wound in his pride that opened up again.

One last shot, and then he stabs the number into the cell phone screen with blunt fingers.

"Raccoon?"

"I'm listening."

"It's a game for kids, this backgammon of yours. You talked about money. I'm warning you: you don't get to fool me twice."

"Fair enough. You know how to play, at least."

"I'm telling you: a kids' game."

"So you're still interested in my offer. Or not?"

"Leave it out, mate. I know your kind. All mouth and no trousers. You're not worth my time."

A sneer in his voice and on his face, for the whole gang to see. You see how I deal with him?

"Of course not," the Raccoon's voice whispers in his ear. "So why exactly are you taking your time to call me?"

A beat. Too hot in this bar. Too much noise. Too many eyes on him. Pretend that the Raccoon is still talking on the other end of the line. Think.

"Yeah, ok then, let's see what you're made of. You come here and we talk. You, me and the rest of the guys."

"Here? That's a little vague."

"The Brewery Tap, in the Lanes."

"It'll have to wait. I'll call you."

Un-fucking-believable. He just hung up. Just like that. Click and he's gone.

"That's right," Tonio says to the dial tone. "I'll be waiting. Believe me, I'll be here waiting for ya."

A U-shaped race. A two-way race, where the players move their pieces in opposite directions. A game of sabotage, where each player tries to remove the other player's pieces from the game so that they have to start from the beginning again.

At the start, the 15 pieces for each player are arranged on 4 of the 24 arrows: 5 on the sixth, 3 on the eighth, 5 on the thirteenth. And the last two, way, way back, right at the beginning of the track, on the twenty-fourth arrow.

It's the dice that regulate the action. The dice decide how many steps a piece can move towards the finish line. And trouble hangs over any piece which finds itself alone on an arrow: if a piece of the opposing colour ends its movement on that same arrow, your miserable defenseless piece is sent back to the beginning of the race again.

A game of dice, so a game of chance.

He was already there. Tonio felt a kick in his stomach when he saw the car outside. Gleaming. Out of place in this run-down part of town. Even parked, it still seemed to be bearing down on him.

Deadeye and the Handyman were drinking their beers on the terrace.

"What you doing here?"

"Watching his wheels. Orders."

The Handyman nodded his head towards the bar. No need to ask whose orders.

Tonio went in. the cardboard game under his arm, the pieces and the dice in the pocket of his jacket. That showed his class, at least, his jacket. His jacket was something else, not like this mockery of a game, even if the guy does have a ride like this one.

The Raccoon had taken his chances with the bar menu. A table in the corner, with a tablecloth, if you please. All wrinkled, of course, with old food stains here and there, but still a tablecloth. And a bottle of wine. Tonio had never seen a bottle like that. Filthy with dust. The landlord should be ashamed of himself.

Tonio sat down opposite. The Raccoon wiped his mouth. Smiled. The same smile as when he had pressed his foot down on the accelerator pedal.

"You've come to play?"

"Cut the bullshit. We're going talk money."

"You know the rules."

The Raccoon put his car key on the table.

"We play 10 games. You win just one game, and the car is yours."

Nobody in the bar who wasn't looking at their table.

"And if you lose them all..."

Blood in his ears like a jackhammer. His eyes locked on the Raccoon's eyes.

"It's a tacky game of chance. There's no way I'll lose them all."

"... you give me your jeans."

Laughter. All the others laughing. Who did they think were they laughing at?

"Of course," the Raccoon's hand was on his car key, "if you don't dare, I'm sure someone else will."

He kept his eyes steady. He ignored Small Fry, behind him, who was squawking "Me! Me!"

Tonio tapped his head. "You've got a screw loose in there, mate. You've got it coming. You've asked for it."

He realised that the game was still under his arm. And that his hands were damp. He put the board on the table. The Raccoon held out his hand to stop him, made a sign to the landlord to clear the table. Poured himself a glass of wine with a smooth practised gesture. Let Tonio place the game pieces.

The gang gathered round them. No, they gathered round Tonio, behind him, at his back. The Raccoon was comfortably settled on his bench, in the corner, facing them all. Waiting for the game to be ready.

"Only four dice?" the Raccoon said.

"Yeah. Why?"

"No matter," said the Raccoon. "It's not important."

The each rolled one dice. Three for the Raccoon, five for Tonio. Five and three. Eight.

The Raccoon took a sip of his wine. Tonio took one of his pieces and moved it forward eight places, tapping on each of the arrows to count out the move. The Raccoon picked up his dice. And waited.

"You're not going to pick up your dice?"

That wasn't in the rules. The rules didn't say that one player had to pick up his dice before the other could play. That's just another of his tricks. A bluff, to unsettle him. But it wasn't going to work. He picked up his dice.

"Go on, then."

Six and one. With no hesitation, the Raccoon played and picked up his dice.

At the end of the first game, the Raccoon said, "My car key is on the table. You should put the belt for your jeans beside it."

And then:

"Perhaps you prefer to use my dice?"

Tonio felt the blood rise to his face. Like little horses, Mary-Lynn had said. If only. Tonio had seen a wall rise up in front of his pieces, an impenetrable wall that prevented his pieces from running, which imprisoned them. Whatever the numbers on the dice that the Raccoon rolled, they brought him luck. Once, or perhaps twice, Tonio had noticed a tiny mark of dissatisfaction on his face. But no matter how hard he looked at the game, he could not understand why.

At the end of the fifth game, the fifth defeat, he couldn't take any more. This guy just sitting there, expressionless, drinking his wine slowly, tilting his head upwards as he swallowed, to savour the flavour fully. And the dice that only brought him bad luck. Tonio asked for a break.

Outside, the night was becoming cool. Deadeye and the Handyman were still there. The only ones who hadn't crowded around to watch him being slaughtered. Nailed to their chairs by God knows what threat or what promise. Backstabbers. He paid no attention to them.

"Tonio?"

Small Fry. What did he want, this little dipstick?

"Tonio, you... you don't see what he's doing?"

"Clear off."

"I can explain to you..."

"Right. Sure. Just beat it."

"Hey, it's your funeral, Tonio."

Swallow your pride. A clue, just one clue, even. Otherwise you'll have lost everything. Your name, your jeans, your credibility. Everything. And the car, too, that goes without saying.

"Wait." Never say his name. That would be showing too much respect. "What do you want?"

"To tell you how he's doing it."

"Well?"

"Backgammon, it's not a game of chance. It's a game of risks. But you can calculate the risks. You can make all the chances play for you. Don't you see that that is what he has been doing all the time?  Look."

Small Fry was holding out a scrap of paper that he had scribbled on. He was holding it out to him. A clue.

"You see? There at the top: 1 2 3 4 5 6. That's what the first dice gives you. And then the column down the side, the same again: 1 2 3 4 5 6. That's what you get from the second dice. And the grid in the middle: 1 and 1, 1 and 2, 1 and 3, all the way up to 6 and 6. That gives you all the possible combinations of numbers. Thirty-six of them. There are 36 combinations, all just as likely."

"So?"

"So you have to take risks, Tonio, otherwise he's going to keep on whipping you. But calculate. Calculate how many combinations he can make that will hurt you. And choose the move that gives him the fewest chances."

Back in the bar, the warmth and the smoke suffocated him. The crowd divided to let him get back to his chair. The Raccoon was lounging back on his bench, relaxing. He watched Tonio through half-closed eyes. The bottle was empty. The key wasn't on the table any more. His belt hadn't moved.

He played better. His scribbled scrap of paper was there, just in front of him when he closed his eyes. He took the time to think. Lost again. And again. And then almost won. Two pieces. He lost by just two frickin' pieces.

The Raccoon, the bitch. But he was going to win it, this dimwit's smart car, with his crackhead game. He was going to be out there, living it up, riding the streets in his own car with the top down and the radio turned all the way up. A car full of girls, all for him. Tonio, the boss man, the big shot.

"The key," he said. "You put it back on the table."

That smile that froze his veins.

The Raccoon put the key down on the table. And a wad of serious banknotes. Would you just take a look at that pile of readies.

"Certainly. But if you lose, you give me everything. Shoes, socks, t-shirt, your flash-boy jacket... everything but your underwear."

"No way, mate. What's your game? You queer or something?"

"You refuse?" The Raccoon was reaching out to take his money.

Around him they were all chanting his name. Tonio! Tonio! Tonio! A meaningless word. But they were on his side, weren't they?

Tonio took hold of the other's arm.

"Leave it."

Applause.

They rolled one dice each. And the Raccoon began.

"One last game? Or would you rather stop there?"

Humiliated. That's what he was. Shattered and humiliated. Not one of his pieces had made it off the board, and there was still one on the bar.

"You..." So many insults seething under his tongue. "... phoney faking liar, you..." Tonio has to turn his head away. "You... you say I'm gonna make a pile of money and you... insult me with this worthless game of shit. Shit!"

It takes all his self-control to stop himself from sending the game board flying, along with all his pathetic hopeless pieces. All his self-control to hold back all the tears that he has been holding in ever since the day his dad walked out when he was just a kid.

"But you can have it all, your pile of money. Or not. It all depends on you."

This liquid voice, the sting of honey, the sugar-coating on a bitter pill.

"You know, I could let you win. The car, and all this." He tapped his fingers on the banknotes. "But this, you won't keep it for long, and the car: you'll soon have it stolen or you'll wrap it round a lamp post. It's not today that you're going to make it rich. Nor tomorrow."

He made a gesture to the landlord to bring his bill.

"We can stop here, if you like. And I'll go. I'll lose interest in you. You'll have shown me that you don't have any class."

He started to lay out the pieces for a new game.

"Or you take this last risk. You win, you lose, it makes no difference to me. You take this one last risk, and then you come and see me. Then perhaps a year from now, maybe two, you'll know how to play. You'll leave this filthy backwater, and you'll set yourself up wherever you want. Paris, Berlin, Miami. You want a car like mine? You'll win enough to buy yourself one in a single evening."

He put the bill beside Tonio's belt, without even looking at it. Holy crap! thought Tonio. What kind of price is that? That much for a poxy bottle of wine and what the short guy in the kitchen thinks will pass for food?

"So... do you dare?"
