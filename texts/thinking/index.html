Faster than thinking

You think that you control your thoughts. You think that you decide what you will do and when you will do it. But neuroscientists have learnt new information about how the brain works. Using brain scanners, they can see the electrical activity in your brain. They think that first your brain makes a decision, and then it tells you about its decision.

Let's try a simple experiment. Touch the end of your nose with your finger. Can you tell me please what you felt first:

  1. Your nose felt that it was being touched by your finger
  2. Your finger felt that it was touching your nose
  3. Both your nose and your finger felt that they were touching at the same time.

Most people say: "Answer number 3". But this is impossible. Messages in your body travel along nerves. They travel quite slowly. The message from the end of your finger takes about 1/4s (a quarter of a second) to travel along your arm and up into your brain. The message from your nose travels along a much shorter path. Your brain receives the message from your nose before it receives the message from your finger. Your brain lies to you. It waits about 1/4s before it tells you "Your nose was touched."

Your brain knows that you touched your nose, but it waits until it receives both messages before it tells your conscious mind about the touch.

The scanners that neuroscientists use work with a technique called "frequency magnetic resonance imaging" (fMRI).

Messages in your brain travel along neurons. A neuron needs oxygen (02) when it is active. Your blood brings oxygen to every part of your body. Your blood is red because it contains a molecule called haemoglobin.

When the haemoglobin is carrying oxygen, it is diamagnetic: it is repelled by a magnet. After it has given its oxygen to a neuron, it becomes paramagnetic: it is attracted by a magnet. (The picture shows the effect of diamagnetism.) An fMRI scanner can "see" where your blood is giving oxygen to your brain cells. It can see which parts of your brain are active at any given time. It is not very accurate, it is not very quick, but it is the best system for looking at your brain without opening your skull. Everybody's brain is different. Dr Adrian Owen has found two thoughts which look very similar in all brains, and which look very different from each other. He puts you into an fMRI scanner and asks you to imagine: * Walking around your house, looking at familiar objects * Standing on a tennis court, playing tennis He asks you to do this many times. He uses a computer program to analyze the information from the scanner. Then he is ready to tell you which of these thoughts you are thinking. In 1999, Scott Routley had a car accident. Since that time, he has been in hospital, in a coma. He cannot talk, he cannot control his muscles. His eyes are open, but he cannot choose to open or close them. He cannot use any part of his body to communicate. In 2012, Dr Owen put him in an fMRI scanner and asked him questions. The answer to each question was "Yes" or "No". He told Scott "If the answer is No, imagine that you are playing tennis. If it is Yes, imagine that you are walking around your house." Scott's mother was with him. She could see the her son's brain scan on a screen. Dr Owen asked Scott many questions, where he knew the answer. "Is your mother called Anne?" "Do you have a brother?" And then he asked him a very important question: "Are you feeling any pain?" The brain image for playing tennis appeared on the scanner. Scott was saying "No. I am not feeling any pain." His mother started to cry with happiness. John-Dylan Haynes uses an fMRI scanner to learn when we make a decision. He puts you in a scanner and he gives you two buttons to press. You can press either button at any time. It is your choice, your decision. You are in control. You see a screen with letters on it. The letters are always changing. When you decide to press a button, you remember what letter you are looking at. But his fMRI scanner is connected to a computer. The computer knows which parts of your brain are active, and it calculates what you are thinking. It predicts which button you are going to press, and when you are going to press it. When you say which letter you were looking at, Dr Haynes knows what time it was when you made your decision. But the computer is able to predict your decision before that letter appeared on the screen. What does this mean? It means that your brain makes the decision for you before you are aware that you have made a decision. Dr Haynes made a game. He put a light beside each button. When the computer calculates that you plan to press a button, it switches on the light for that button. Your task is to press a button, before the computer makes its prediction, when the light for that button is off. The computer wins 60% of the time. Are you controlling your thoughts, or are they controlling you?
